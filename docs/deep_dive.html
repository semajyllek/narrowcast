Technical reference · September 2026

Narrow-catalogue classification over frozen encoders

How to build an image classifier over a short, hand-picked list of labels — the 20 plants in one garden, the defect types on one production line — on top of an encoder you never train, and how to decide when it should say "I don't know" instead of guessing. The finding this document exists for is that the two numbers people normally publish about such a model can both improve while the model gets worse, in a way that is reproducible, predictable, and easy to ship by accident.

The first result, stated without jargon

The model always gives one of three replies: name a label ("that is Sedum album"), back off to the group ("that is some kind of Sedum"), or decline ("I don't know").

Build it twice. First over 14 plant species from 14 different genera. Then over 14 species drawn from just two genera — 8 Sedum, 6 Trifolium — with the same training images, the same encoder, and the same fitting procedure. Only the relatedness of the chosen labels changes.

The two numbers almost everyone reports both say the second model is fine:

Precisionhow often a reply it gives is true98.5%97.8%unchanged

Both would pass review. But the second model is the worse product, and two numbers that usually go unreported say so:

Where the extra coverage came from: the crowded model answers "it is a Sedum" far more often. That reply is true, so precision counts it as correct, and it is a reply, so coverage counts it as answered — but when 8 of the user's 14 labels are Sedum, it has narrowed almost nothing. The metric improves because the answer got less useful.

The second result: it is predictable, but only relative to a stated assumption

How far a model will back off like this can be estimated before it ships, from a quantity called headroom — how much more often the model gets the group right than the species right, measured on held-out calibration data. Across 1,409 model/label-set combinations spanning plants, birds, text and audio, headroom predicts the amount of backing-off at cross-validated R² 0.883, against 0.362 for accuracy alone.

That rule is incomplete, and §11 shows how. It ignores how often the user is expected to photograph something that is not on the list at all — a rate the builder declares up front. Hold headroom fixed and sweep only that rate from 5% to 60%: the backing-off the rule is trying to predict moves from 13.8% of observations to 0.15%, a factor of 91, while the rule's own prediction sits unchanged at 22.3% the whole time. The prediction is meaningless until that assumption is named.

Scope

Covered: the decision rule and its calibration, the evaluation protocol, the metric pathology this work exists to expose, and the experiments that established or killed each claim.

Treated as a black box: the encoders themselves. BioCLIP-2, MobileCLIP2 and PlantCLEF2024 are used frozen, never fine-tuned, and are documented only by their input/output contract — an image in, an L2-normalised vector out. Distillation into a smaller encoder was attempted and failed; see §13.

Code: narrowcast (the tool), plantid (the research record), and the demo repos that consume the installed package — narrowcast-kws (audio) and narrowcast-derm (medical imaging). Every formula below is transcribed from those sources.

Reference

Notation and terms

Everything below is defined once here and used consistently. The two thresholds and headroom carry most of the weight, and all three are easy to misread.

The two scores, computed per image

symbolnamewhat it is
slabel_confthe largest single-label posterior — how sure the model is of its best guess
γgroup_confthe largest group mass: sum the posteriors of all labels sharing a group, take the biggest such sum

s ≤ γ always (proved in §4), which is what lets two independent thresholds define a well-ordered three-way decision.

The two thresholds, fitted once per model

symbolcompared againstwhat crossing it means
tgroupγthe decline threshold. If γ < tgroup the model refuses to answer at all. Fitted mostly to reject things outside the label set — so it moves with the assumed out-of-set rate, which is the subject of §11.
tlabelsthe retreat threshold. If γ passed but s < tlabel, the model declines to name a label and answers at group rank instead.
The most common misreading

t_group is not "the threshold for giving a group answer". It is the threshold for answering at all. A group answer requires passing t_group and failing t_label — it lives in the band between them. When t_group rises, that band is squeezed from below and would-be group answers become declines. That single fact is the mechanism in §11.

The three outcomes, and the shares that report them

Every in-set observation ends in exactly one of three states, so these three shares sum to 1:

termdefinition
label-level sharefraction of in-set observations answered at the fine rank (a specific label named). The only one of these that falls when the label set is crowded.
group sharefraction answered at the coarse rank only — the retreat. Called group-answer share interchangeably.
decline sharefraction refused outright.
coveragefraction of all queries answered at either rank — prevalence-weighted across in-set and out-of-set buckets. Counts a group answer exactly like a label answer, which is why it flatters crowded sets.
precisionfraction of answers that are correct, counting a correct group answer as correct.
closed-set top-1plain accuracy on in-set rows, ignoring the cascade entirely.

Headroom

headroom = (coarse-rank accuracy) − (fine-rank accuracy) Both measured on the calibration split, on in-set rows only, unweighted. Coarse accuracy = how often the true group is top-ranked; fine accuracy = how often the true label is. In code: group_ok.mean() − label_ok.mean().

Read it as: how much easier is the coarse question than the fine one? At headroom 0.194 (the 20 Newsgroups arm) a group answer is right 19.4pp more often than a label answer, so retreating raises expected utility. At headroom 0.000 the group rank is exactly as hard and retreating trades 1.0 for 0.5 with no change in the odds of being wrong.

Two properties matter later and are easy to miss:

  • Headroom is a property of the label set and encoder, not of the thresholds. It is computed from which rows the head gets right, before any threshold is applied.
  • Headroom is invariant to the assumed out-of-set rate, because no deployment weight enters it. This is exactly what makes the experiment in §11 clean: it pins the prediction while the thing being predicted moves.
Headroom is bounded above by 1 − fine accuracy

And it equals that bound whenever every label shares one group, because then the coarse question has a single possible answer and coarse accuracy is exactly 1.000. A one-group label set therefore has no measurable headroom — it has an identity. This sinks any experiment built on such a set, and it is why the crowded arms throughout this document always have two or more groups.

Buckets and prevalence

termdefinition
in_catalogheld-out rows of the chosen labels
near_oodrows of labels outside the set that share a group with it — the hard negatives
distant_oodunrelated negatives, supplied as background
poodthe assumed share of real queries that are not on the label set. Declared, never measured from the evaluation set. See §6.
clusterthe unit that must not straddle a train/test split — several photographs of one subject. Also the unit resampled by the bootstrap, which is one column doing two jobs; see §11.

01

The problem

A general-purpose identifier answers over a very large label space — iNaturalist's vision model covers ~108,000 taxa. A narrow-catalogue classifier answers over a label set someone chose: the twenty plants in a garden, the defect classes on one production line, the birds at one feeder.

Narrowing raises accuracy: on a label set spanning distinct groups, going from 50 labels to 10 moves top-1 from 0.939 to 0.989. But it introduces a failure mode absent from the wide-label-space setting — the model can answer at a coarser rank than the one the user asked about — and coverage and precision both rise as that happens.

The setting

Given a chosen label set L = {ℓ₁ … ℓ_K}, a group function g(ℓ) mapping each label to a coarser rank, and images that may or may not depict something in L, produce for each image one of three outcomes: name the label, name the group, or decline.

The group rank is the load-bearing part. It gives the model a third option between naming a label and refusing — "it is an oak, I cannot tell which" — which is informative when the set holds two oaks and vacuous when it holds twelve. Coverage and precision score both cases identically.

02

Architecture: what is actually built per user

The encoder is frozen and shared. What gets built per label set is a multinomial logistic head plus two scalar thresholds.

φ : image → ℝd  (frozen, never updated)
z = φ(x) / ‖φ(x)‖₂
P(c | x) = softmax(W z + b)c,  W ∈ ℝ(K+1)×d The extra row is __OTHER__, a reject class fitted on supplied negatives. d = 512 or 768 depending on encoder.

This is the whole personalisation story, and it explains the economics. At K = 20 and d = 512 the head is 21 × 512 × 4 bytes ≈ 43 KB, against an encoder of 17.9–152 MB. Fitting it is a few CPU-seconds on cached embeddings.

Consequence for anyone sizing a deployment

Choosing fewer labels does not produce a smaller model. Going from ~700 labels to 20 shrinks the head from 1.5 MB to 43 KB and leaves the encoder unchanged: on a 152 MB bundle that is 1.0% of the total falling to 0.03%. A tool promising "pick 20 classes, get a tiny model" is promising something this architecture cannot deliver.

The reject class is optional and its absence is reported, not silently assumed away:

if background is not None:
    B = _vecs(background)
    cut = rng.permutation(len(B))
    n = int(BG_TRAIN_FRAC * len(B))          # 0.6 to train, 0.4 held out as far-OOD
    Xtr.append(B[cut[:n]]); ytr.append(np.full(n, OTHER))
else:
    notes.append("no background supplied: closed-set only, the model cannot decline")

03

The three-way cascade

Two scalars are computed per image. s is the top label posterior; γ is the largest group mass, obtained by summing label posteriors within each group.

G ∈ {0,1}M×K,  Gj,i = 1 ⟺ g(ℓi) = group j
p = [P(ℓ₁|x) … P(ℓ_K|x)]  (the non-OTHER columns)

s = maxi pi
γ = maxj (G pT)j = maxj Σi ∈ group j pi G is built once per model by group_matrix; γ is a matrix product, not a loop.

The decision is two thresholded comparisons, evaluated in a fixed order:

def decide(label_conf, group_conf, t_group, t_label):
    out = np.full(len(label_conf), LABEL, dtype=object)
    out[label_conf < t_label] = GROUP      # not confident enough to name the label
    out[group_conf < t_group] = DECLINE    # not confident enough even at group

Order matters: the second assignment overwrites the first, so DECLINE dominates. Written as a rule:

decide(s, γ) = DECLINE  if γ < tgroup
             = GROUP    if γ ≥ tgroup and s < tlabel
             = LABEL    otherwise 
stateDiagram-v2
    [*] --> Scored: compute s, γ
    Scored --> DECLINE: γ < t_group
    Scored --> Confident: γ ≥ t_group
    Confident --> GROUP: s < t_label
    Confident --> LABEL: s ≥ t_label
    DECLINE --> [*]
    GROUP --> [*]
    LABEL --> [*]

There are three reachable states and no others. Critically, the state "confident at label, unsure at group" is unreachable — which is not enforced by the code but guaranteed by a property of the scores themselves.

04

Why the scores nest

The cascade is well-ordered because s ≤ γ always. The proof is one line, and it is the reason the rule can be two independent thresholds rather than a joint region.

s = maxi pi = pi*  where i* = argmaxi pi
Let j* = g(ℓi*). Then:

γ ≥ Σi ∈ group j* pi ≥ pi* = s The first inequality because γ is a max over groups and j* is one of them; the second because every pi ≥ 0. Equality holds iff the top label is alone in its group.

And the chain extends upward:

s ≤ γ ≤ Σi pi = 1 − P(__OTHER__ | x)

Worked example

K = 5 labels over 2 groups. Posterior over the non-OTHER columns:

labelgrouppi
Sedum acreSedum0.31
Sedum albumSedum0.34
Sedum dasyphyllumSedum0.22
Bellis perennisBellis0.08
Bellis sylvestrisBellis0.05

s = 0.34 (Sedum album). γ = max(0.31+0.34+0.22, 0.08+0.05) = 0.87 (Sedum). With t_group = 0.70, t_label = 0.55: γ = 0.87 ≥ 0.70 so it does not decline; s = 0.34 < 0.55 so it answers "Sedum".

This is the correct behaviour under the declared utilities, and it is also the failure mode. The answer is true, and it counts as one answered query and one correct answer in both headline metrics. But three of these five labels are Sedum, so it has narrowed five candidates to three — and coverage and precision cannot distinguish that from narrowing five to one.

05

Declared utilities

Thresholds are fitted by maximising expected utility. The payoffs are written down before any fitting, in source, and changing them is a deliberate act.

UTILITY = {"label_correct": 1.0, "group_correct": 0.5, "wrong": -4.0,
           "decline_ood": 1.0, "decline_in_catalog": 0.0}
U(decision, truth) =
  +1.0  LABEL and correct
  +0.5  GROUP and group correct
  −4.0  any answer that is wrong
  +1.0  DECLINE on something outside the label set  (the right call)
   0.0  DECLINE on something inside it  (a miss, not an error)

The implementation is branchless so the grid search can call it tens of thousands of times:

is_dec, is_sp = levels == DECLINE, levels == LABEL
is_gn = ~is_dec & ~is_sp
return (
    is_dec * np.where(in_catalog, w["decline_in_catalog"], w["decline_ood"])
    + is_sp * np.where(label_ok,  w["label_correct"], w["wrong"])
    + is_gn * np.where(group_ok,  w["group_correct"], w["wrong"])
)
Why declare rather than optimise a metric

An "is the answer useful" objective is degenerate here. Group accuracy is always higher than label accuracy — 0.974 vs 0.837 on the 490-class catalogue — so any metric rewarding correctness alone is maximised by always answering group, which deletes the product while scoring beautifully.

The wrong: −4.0 figure was raised from −2.0 once thresholds began being fitted at a stated prevalence. The frontier it moves along, on held-out data: μ=2 gives precision 0.944 at coverage 0.797; μ=4 gives 0.965 at 0.747; μ=32 gives 0.991 at 0.645 but label-level answering falls to 0.126 — that is, 99.1% precision is reached while naming a label on 12.6% of in-set observations.

Known limitation

group_correct is a flat 0.5 regardless of how much the group narrows the field. On a label set where every member shares one group, a group answer conveys zero information and still scores 0.5. This is why the crowded arm in §9 retreats instead of declining, and so why its coverage rises. A group-informativeness term would fix it — and by this project's own conventions it must be declared before being fitted, so it remains open rather than quietly patched.

06

Prevalence anchoring

The operating point depends on how often a user photographs something outside their label set. That rate is unknown, and it is the single largest lever on every reported number. Leaving it to the evaluation set's incidental composition means the sampling accident chooses the product.

Given buckets bi ∈ {in_catalog, near_ood, distant_ood} and assumed out-of-set rate p:

wi = (1 − p) / nin           if bi = in_catalog
wi = p · (mb / Σm) / nb   if bi = b, an OOD bucket

then rescaled: w ← w · N / Σw mb is the assumed share of OOD traffic in bucket b (near/distant = 0.32/0.68); nb is how many rows the evaluation set happens to contain. Dividing by nb is what removes the sampling accident.
Why this exists — a real incident

Thresholds were once fitted on whatever mix the evaluation set contained, then reported under a different assumption. Expanding the in-catalogue bucket from 750 to 2,283 observations — to settle an unrelated question — moved the set from 59.5% out-of-catalogue to 44.8%. That alone moved t_label from 0.897 to 0.552 and took label-level answers from 9% of in-catalogue observations to 67%.

Adding data, changing nothing else, produced a completely different product. Both fitting and reporting are now anchored to a stated p.

07

Fitting the thresholds

Exhaustive grid search over the quantiles of the observed score distributions. Non-differentiable objective, two parameters, so this is exact rather than approximate.

(tgroup*, tlabel*) = argmax(tg, tℓ) Σi ŵi · U(decide(si, γi, tg, tℓ), truthi) ŵ = w / Σw, the normalised deployment weights. Grid points are the 60 quantiles of γ and of s over the calibration split — so the search adapts to the score distribution rather than assuming it spans [0,1]. 
g_grid = np.quantile(group_conf, np.linspace(0, 1, n_grid))   # n_grid = 60
s_grid = np.quantile(label_conf, np.linspace(0, 1, n_grid))
best, best_u = (0.0, 0.0), -np.inf
for tg in g_grid:
    for ts in s_grid:
        u = float(np.dot(utility(decide(label_conf, group_conf, tg, ts),
                                 label_ok, group_ok, in_catalog, weights), sw))
        if u > best_u:
            best, best_u = (float(tg), float(ts)), u

3,600 evaluations, each vectorised over the whole calibration split. Fitted on the calibration half only; the test half is never touched by threshold selection.

A degenerate case worth recognising

On a group-crowded label set the fitted thresholds become invariant to p. Measured on an all-Sedum/Trifolium set: t_group = 0.1173, t_label = 0.9065, identical at p = 0.5, 0.2 and 0.1, while coverage moved 0.686 → 0.933.

This is not a bug and not a grid artifact — t_label = 0.9065 is an interior optimum against a maximum observed s of 0.9903, and on well-separated sets the same fit does respond to p (t_group 0.8513 → 0.3973). The mechanism: when the near-OOD bucket consists of species from groups the user chose, a group answer is correct for in-catalogue and near-OOD rows alike. Group answering therefore earns utility at any prevalence, t_group sits near the floor, and declining is never worth it. Coverage still moves with p because the bucket weights move, not the thresholds.

08

Evaluation discipline

Cluster, never row

Several photographs commonly depict the same subject. Treating them as independent inflates effective sample size and produces intervals that do not replicate — which happened twice in this project's history before the rule was adopted.

def make_splits(df, seed=0):
    for bucket, group in df.groupby("bucket"):
        key = SPLIT_CLUSTER.get(bucket, "label")   # near_ood splits on GROUP, not label
        clusters = np.array(sorted(group[key].unique()))
        rng.shuffle(clusters)
        calib = set(clusters[: len(clusters) // 2])

The per-bucket key matters. Splitting near-OOD by label would put two species of the same group on opposite sides, and the decision being fitted there is the group decision — so near-OOD splits on group.

A bug this caused, and how it surfaced

Background rows were once labelled __OTHER__ in the clustering column as well as the label column. make_splits therefore saw a single cluster, and clusters[:1//2] is empty — so all 1,554 background rows went to test and none to calibration. Thresholds were being fitted with no distant negatives at all.

Fixing it moved calibration from 562 rows to 1,402, coverage 0.753 → 0.618 and precision 0.961 → 0.985. The clustering identity now carries the real label for every row including background, while truth keeps __OTHER__ so correctness flags stay false.

Bootstrap the ratio, not the mean

Coverage and precision are prevalence-weighted across buckets. Bootstrapping the unweighted mean of the same rows estimates a different quantity entirely.

θ̂ = Σi νi / Σi δi For precision: ν = w·1[answered]·1[correct], δ = w·1[answered]. For coverage: ν = w·1[answered], δ = w. Resample clusters with replacement, recompute the ratio within each resample, take the 2.5/97.5 percentiles.
Caught by construction

The first implementation bootstrapped np.mean(correct[answered]) — an unweighted mean — and produced a 95% interval of 22.3–77.1% around a point estimate of 96.1%. Unweighted, precision is dominated by the OOD rows that deployment_weights exists to down-weight. The defect was visible only because the interval was printed beside the number it contradicted.

09

The coarse-answer trap

This is the central result. Two 14-label sets, drawn from the same catalogue, encoded by the same model, built and measured identically. One is crowded — 8 Sedum and 6 Trifolium, two groups. The other spans 14 distinct groups.

metriccrowded (2 groups)separated (14 groups)reading

Precision0.9780.985indistinguishable
Label-level share0.4760.76128.5pp fewer named
Closed-set top-10.8170.97015pp worse when it does name

The crowded set answers 30% more queries at the same precision, names a label on 28.5pp fewer of them, and is 15.3pp less accurate when it does name one. The extra coverage is bought with group answers that narrow nothing — "it is a Sedum" when eight of fourteen labels are Sedum.

Why coverage inverts

Recall from §4 that γ ≥ s, with equality only when the top label is alone in its group. Crowding does two things at once:

  1. γ rises. Probability mass that was split across siblings sums back together at group level, so γ climbs toward 1 and the decline test γ < t_group almost never fires.
  2. s falls. That same splitting depresses the top label posterior, so s < t_label fires often and the answer retreats to group.

Coverage counts any non-decline, so it rises. Precision counts group answers as correct, so it holds. Only the label-level share — the fraction of in-set observations answered at the fine rank — records what happened.

It is not a plant finding

Prediction registered in advance, then tested on birds through the same encoder: Larus (7 gulls) + Calidris (6 sandpipers) against 13 distinct genera.

The coverage rise is far smaller on birds (+1.8pp against +18.8pp), so the coverage inflation is not a fixed quantity; the label-level collapse is what reproduces. Larus and Calidris are the groups birders themselves rank hardest, so the crowding is not contrived.

What this licenses

Stating the trap as a property of hierarchical label sets, not of plant taxonomy — which is what justifies shipping the structural warnings into any domain. It does not license the numbers, which were measured on one domain through one encoder. In the tool, projection is disabled unless a measured profile exists for the requested domain; there is no fallback to the plant grid.

10

Headroom: predicting the retreat

§9 establishes that crowded label sets retreat to the group rank and that this is invisible in coverage and precision. The obvious next question is whether the amount of retreat can be predicted from the calibration split, before the test half is touched. It can, to cross-validated R² 0.883, and the predictor is headroom — coarse-rank accuracy minus fine-rank accuracy.

h = accuracy(coarse rank) − accuracy(fine rank)  on in-set calibration rows

group share ≈ 1.8 · h  — as a floor, not a point estimate

Fitted over 1,409 arms spanning plants, birds, text and two audio corpora, headroom predicts group-answer share at cross-validated R² = 0.883, against 0.362 for fine accuracy alone. It began as a hypothesis at n = 4 and survived the scale-up.

The controlled comparison

The cleanest evidence holds everything fixed but the grouping. One fitted head, fine accuracy pinned at 0.659, only the group column varying: group answers move 0.022 → 0.417 and coverage 0.200 → 0.500. The grouping alone moves the product, with the classifier untouched.

Two audio corpora, and what they decided

The audio rows above are not padding — they are where the rule's limits were found, and they cost two wrong turns worth recording.

Semantic groups are not groups. The first keyword-spotting arms grouped words by meaning — digit, direction, response. Measured against wav2vec2's own geometry those are barely groups at all: mean within-group cosine +0.942 for digit and +0.919 for direction, against +0.917 for all word pairs. "Direction" is 0.002 above chance. The encoder's actual neighbours are no/one, six/yes, tree/two — phonetic, not semantic. Group mass never summed, γ never rose, and the cascade correctly never used the group rank. Headroom 0.047, group share 0.031.

Regrouping by the encoder's own geometry restores it. Agglomerative clustering on the embeddings produced a genuine /f/-onset cluster — {five, follow, forward, four, off} — and headroom rose to 0.084 with group share 0.150. Same words, same encoder, same head: only the group column changed.

ESC-50 is the harder instrument — 2,000 clips over 50 classes, 40 per class, with documented within-category confusions (rain / sea_waves / water_drops are all water). Its crowded arm is genuinely harder: closed-set top-1 falls 1.000 → 0.900. And every metric moves the honest way — coverage down, precision down, label share down. There is no trap there, because there is nothing hidden.

Headroom predicts retreat, not harm

This is the sharpest thing the audio work established. Retreat and harm are different quantities, and headroom only predicts the first. The crowded-set warning is therefore a warning about a risk, not a diagnosis — which is exactly why no report here ever drops the label-level share, and why the tool's card gates on measured retreat rather than on predicted retreat.

Two honest qualifications, and a third in §11

Headroom is nearly but not exactly the governing quantity: tested in raw units, b + c = +0.430 with a CI excluding zero, meaning coarse accuracy weighs about 25% more than fine. And below the 0.889 break-even retreat is suppressed rather than eliminated. These are one story — coarse gets a second bite through the threshold.

Test that comparison in raw units. With unequal standard deviations, b + c = 0 on z-scores tests a different hypothesis and reports the asymmetry with the wrong sign. It did, in a first draft.

Where does 0.889 come from? Directly from the declared utilities in §5. Retreating to the group on an in-set row earns 0.5 when the group is right and −4.0 when it is not; declining earns 0.0. So retreat is worth it only when group accuracy q satisfies:

0.5·q − 4.0·(1 − q) > 0  ⟺  q > 4.0 / 4.5 = 0.889

11

What headroom omits: the operating point

The 1.8 × rule was calibrated on arms where retreat actually happens. Applying it to a new domain exposed a variable it does not carry — and the variable is not the domain.

Setting. 2,688 clinical dermatology photographs from Fitzpatrick17k (Atlas Dermatologico rows), 86 conditions, embedded with facebook/dinov2-base — a general self-supervised encoder with no dermatology supervision and no fine-tuning on this corpus. The crowded arm is six conditions in two groups (4 inflammatory + 2 genodermatoses); the varied arm is six conditions in six groups.

Headroom is constant to machine precision down that column — verified per seed and per fit domain, maximum spread 0.0. Meanwhile the thing it predicts ranges over 0.0015–0.1383, a factor of 91, and sits below the floor at every operating point — by 0.084 at pood = 0.05 and by 0.221 at 0.60.

The mechanism: t_group squeezes the retreat band

A group answer requires γ ≥ tgroup and s < tlabel — it lives in the band between the two thresholds. t_group is fitted mostly as an out-of-set rejection threshold, so it climbs with pood (0.535 → 0.835) and eats that band from below. Rows that should have retreated are declined instead.

Measured directly: of in-set rows failing t_label — the rows that should retreat — between 59% and 93% also fail t_group and are declined.

So the corrected statement:

headroom predicts the retreat that is available.
pood and encoder strength decide how much is realised.

This is a boundary condition, not a fact about dermatology. The dermatology arm ran at closed-set top-1 ≈ 0.75, against 0.84–0.97 for the plant arms the rule was fitted on. A weak encoder or a high out-of-set rate reaches the same place in any domain — plants included. The sweep exists specifically to separate "this domain is different" from "this operating point is different", and it separates them.

Practical consequence

Do not quote 1.8 × headroom as an expected group share without naming an operating point. The tool already gates its card on measured retreat rather than on headroom; that decision is what this result vindicates.

The population-shift test, and why it is reported as a null

The same corpus carries an annotator-assigned skin type per image, allowing a model to be fitted on one population and evaluated on another inside a single atlas — same photographer, same equipment, same label vocabulary. Train and calibration rows are shared between each control and its shifted twin, so headroom is identical within a pair and only the test population moves.

The two hardest failures are in-domain controls, so the failure above is not a shift effect at all — which is what sent the investigation to the pood sweep.

Declared in advance, and it fired

The pre-registration stated that skin type within one atlas is a mild shift, and that a top-1 drop smaller than the control's own seed-to-seed range would mean the rule was never stressed. Only one of four shifted conditions cleared that bar (crowded dark→light, drop 0.109 against a control seed range of 0.106). So the shift question is unanswered, not answered.

Without that line written down first, "three of four shifted conditions behaved" was an available and false story. The control also held exactly as specified: the varied arm's headroom was 0.000 in all 40 runs, asserted in code rather than interpreted, since one label per group makes coarse accuracy identical to fine by construction.

One column, two jobs

cluster is used both to keep a subject's photographs on one side of a split and as the bootstrap resampling unit. Here the natural split unit is the near-duplicate clique (~45–290 per arm), which gives tight intervals while condition difficulty — the dominant variance component — goes unresampled. Every figure above therefore reports a second interval resampling the six conditions. Quoting the clique interval alone would understate the uncertainty severalfold.

Safety: per-pair vs. union

When being wrong is expensive, the usual practice is to enumerate the dangerous confusions and measure each. That is necessary and not sufficient: below, no single declared pair exceeds 2.5% while their union reaches 6.7% against a 1% bar — so a per-pair report passes a model the union report fails.

6.7% against a 1% bar declared in advance, while no individual pair exceeded 2.5%. The errors scatter across many different harmless-looking labels, each individually small. A per-pair report passes a model a union report fails.

The pre-registration said never average across pairs — averaging hides the worst case — and that stands. But a union is not an average, and it was missing. Both are required.

Abstention resolves it: at 75% coverage the rate falls to 0.8% and at 50% it is zero. Refusing a quarter of hemlock photographs is correct product behaviour.

Group answers count as dangerous

A first implementation counted only label-level answers. That is wrong: "it is a Lomatium" for poison hemlock is precisely the error that kills foragers, because Lomatium roots are dug and eaten. A coarse answer naming a group containing nothing hazardous is as actionable as a wrong label. Only declining — or answering with the hazard's own group — is safe.

In the tool this is a gate, not a statistic. Labels the user declares consequential are checked against a bar fixed in advance, and the card refuses to certify a model that exceeds it.

The size/accuracy frontier

How much does a smaller encoder cost as the label set narrows? 530-species catalogue, frozen encoders, 15 draws per point, random (mostly cross-group) subsets.

The penalty shrinks monotonically as the catalogue narrows: 4.0 → 1.6pp for S2 between K=50 and K=10. One confound was checked, since the reject class draws on a fixed pool and the label:background ratio therefore shifts ~5× across this range. Halving background at K=10 moved the gaps 1.6 → 1.2pp and 3.4 → 3.5pp, so the trend tracks encoder capacity rather than fit composition.

Retracted in place

These numbers are measured on a curated, plant-centred corpus. Repeating the K=20 cell on real iNaturalist field photographs: BioCLIP-2 0.9790 → 0.9769 (−0.2pp), MobileCLIP2-S2 0.9493 → 0.8557 (−9.4pp). The gap quadruples, from 3.0pp to 12.1pp.

The frontier is an upper bound for the small encoders specifically. It is not wrong — it is measured on the data it names — but it does not survive a change of image source, and a user's photographs resemble field photographs.

PlantCLEF2024 has a third of BioCLIP-2's parameters and nearly twice its latency, because it runs at 518px — 5.3× the pixels. So a byte budget and a latency budget rank these differently, and the tool's choose() ranks storage only and says so.

The middle ground is not a distilled model

Distilling BioCLIP-2 into a ViT-B — exactly this size class — was run on an A100 over 48k transfer images, reaching held-out cosine 0.956 to the teacher. It lost 0.7pp of group accuracy against the off-the-shelf model it was meant to beat, recovering ~10% of a 19pp coverage gap. Compressing the large encoder does not work; choosing a different one does.

This is also one of three data points behind a standing rule in this project: cosine similarity to a teacher does not predict downstream accuracy. It bounds how much could have changed and says nothing about whether what changed mattered.

Measuring contamination

BioCLIP-2 trains on iNaturalist via GBIF, and the evaluation images are iNaturalist. Every number was therefore hedged as an upper bound. That hedge is now measured.

Design. BioCLIP-2 was released June 2025, so its GBIF snapshot predates that. Observations uploaded after mid-2025 are image-level novel to it while being the same platform, photographers and species. Two disjoint test sets, same training corpus, 20 common Oregon plants:

The large encoders are indistinguishable from zero. Whatever memorisation advantage exists is bounded under one percentage point.

The control is the finding

A first attempt compared post-cutoff against the existing training corpus and found post-cutoff scoring higher for all three encoders — which would have supported a much stronger claim. That was a sampling artifact: the corpus had been drawn with order_by=votes, and heavily-faved observations are not a random sample. Re-drawing the pre-cutoff side identically (order_by=created_at desc) reversed the sign and shrank the effect to noise.

The drop belongs to the small encoder, and it is not memorisation. MobileCLIP2-S2 loses 2.3pp with a CI excluding zero — the largest drop, from the encoder least likely to have trained on iNaturalist. It is sensitivity to drift, and it is the same pattern as the image-source change in §13 where S2 lost 9.4pp and BioCLIP-2 lost 0.2pp. Two independent measurements agree: a small encoder's accuracy is not merely lower, it is less durable.

Failure modes and open questions

Known failure modes

  • Narrowing does not fix a confusable set. Going 50 → 10 labels buys ~4pp on a group-dense set (0.710 → 0.752) against 0.939 → 0.989 on a separated one. The tool warns; it cannot solve.
  • Near-OOD is the weakest bucket. Siblings of chosen labels that were left outside the set have no correct answer available. On one build, only 16.9% were answered and 59.6% of those were correct — against 98.1% on in-set rows.
  • Group answers can be vacuous. §9. The flat 0.5 utility is the proximate cause.
  • Retreat can be silently swallowed by declining. §11. At a high assumed out-of-set rate or a weak encoder, t_group rises into the band where group answers live and 59–93% of would-be retreats become declines. Headroom does not see this, so the 1.8 × rule over-promises by up to 70× at fixed headroom.
  • One cluster column serves two purposes. Splitting wants the finest honest subject grouping; the bootstrap wants the coarsest meaningful one. When they differ, the reported interval is the split unit's and can be far too tight.
  • The reference pool is a floor. Sibling warnings check against a bundled 499-binomial list. A group whose relatives that list never included looks safer than it is — and at a pool of thousands the check inverts, since nearly every label would have outside relatives and "shares a group" stops carrying information. Ranking by embedding distance is the fix.

Open questions

  1. Domain shift — still open, and one attempt did not settle it. Contamination is measured and small; the trap generalises across kingdoms; but every plant number in both repos comes from iNaturalist photographs, and nobody has pointed a different camera at a plant and asked. The dermatology population shift in §11 was built to attack the general version of this and produced a declared null: the axis moved accuracy by less than seed noise in three of four conditions. A genuinely different acquisition source — a second atlas, or consumer phone photographs against clinical ones — remains the experiment worth running. The obvious candidate, splitting Fitzpatrick17k across its two source atlases, is currently unrunnable: DermaAmin supplies 12,631 of its 16,577 rows and that host is suspended.
  2. An operating-point term in the retreat rule. §11 shows group share is jointly determined by headroom and pood. A two-variable rule is the obvious next form, and it needs arms that vary both — the 1,409 existing arms were nearly all fitted at pood = 0.2.
  3. A group-informativeness utility term. Would fix the vacuous-group degeneracy. Must be declared before being fitted.
  4. A --holdout-column in the tool. §11's experiment required monkeypatching make_splits, because narrowcast assigns calibration and test at random — everywhere it had been applied, both came from one population. Making the split column declarable would let any user ask "how does this behave on a population I did not calibrate on" without patching the library.
  5. Core ML export of PlantCLEF2024. The 38.6 ms is MPS. 518px is a very different graph on the Neural Engine, and int4 per-grouped-channel is known to be silently wrong on the Metal GPU backend in this stack — returning a plausible unit-norm embedding while being incorrect. The deployment claim for the 43 MB option is unverified on-device.
  6. Encoder generality. The bird replication used the same encoder. The trap is established across label hierarchy and visual domain, not across encoders or data sources.
